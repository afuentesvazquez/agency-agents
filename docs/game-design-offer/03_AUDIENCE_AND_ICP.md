# Audience & ICP Analysis

Low-ticket product ($19/$39) that converts a vague game concept into structured design artifacts. Helps sharpen a raw idea, pressure-test viability, define core mechanic/loop, identify risks, and reach pitch-ready or prototype-ready state faster.

This document ranks candidate audiences by fit, dissects their psychology, and recommends who to build for first.

---

## Candidate Segments at a Glance

| # | Segment | Fit Rank | Verdict |
|---|---------|----------|---------|
| 1 | Solo indie creator | **A -- Primary ICP** | Highest urgency, highest volume, lowest resistance to price point |
| 2 | Programmer with a game idea | **A- -- Strong Secondary** | Clear pain, but needs careful framing so it doesn't feel like "you lack creativity" |
| 3 | Small team in concept phase (2-5) | **B+ -- Tertiary / Expansion** | Real need for alignment artifacts, but buying dynamics are murkier |
| 4 | First-time creative lead | **B -- Situational** | Narrow window of pain; good upsell audience later, weak cold-acquisition target |
| 5 | Aspiring game designer (student/career-changer) | **C -- Deprioritize** | Wants education, not output. Will consume free content forever before paying. Misaligned motivation. |

## Segment Deep Dives

### 1. Solo Indie Creator (Primary ICP)

**Who they are.**
One person (or functionally one person with occasional contractor help) trying to ship a game. Age 22-40. May or may not code -- could be a programmer, artist, musician, or narrative designer wearing all hats. Has shipped zero to two small projects. Works evenings/weekends or recently went full-time indie. Active on itch.io, indie dev Twitter/Bluesky, r/gamedev, or small Discord communities.

**Skill level:** Intermediate in at least one craft discipline. Novice-to-nonexistent in structured game design methodology.

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **What they already know** | They have taste. They can identify games they love and loosely articulate why. They understand basic game dev workflows (engine, assets, build). They know the market is brutal. |
| **What they are bad at** | Scoping. Saying no to features. Articulating their core loop in one sentence. Distinguishing "interesting to me" from "viable as a product." Translating an aesthetic vision into mechanical decisions. Killing darlings before sunk cost sets in. |
| **What they fear** | Spending 2 years on something nobody wants. Realizing mid-development that the core concept is broken. Being seen as "just another person with an idea." Shipping something mediocre. Running out of savings/motivation before finishing. |
| **Why they buy this product** | It gives them a forcing function. They know they should do this thinking before coding, but they won't do it unprompted. A structured artifact makes the fuzzy thing in their head feel real and testable. At $19-39 it's an impulse buy relative to the anxiety it addresses. |
| **What they will NOT pay for** | Theory. A game design textbook repackaged. Anything that feels like homework without output. Anything that positions them as beginners -- they already have a craft. Generic "business plan" templates that ignore games specifically. |
| **Language that resonates** | "Sharpen your concept before you burn months building it." "Go from 'I have this idea...' to a design doc you can actually build from." "Pressure-test your game idea in a weekend." "Scope it, spec it, ship it." |

### 2. Programmer with a Game Idea (Strong Secondary)

**Who they are.**
Software developer (often web, mobile, or backend) with 2-10+ years of professional coding experience. Has always wanted to make a game. May have completed a game jam entry or two. Age 25-45. Comfortable in Unity/Godot/Unreal at a technical level but has never designed a game from scratch at a conceptual level. Over-indexes on "what engine/framework" questions and under-indexes on "what is the actual game" questions.

**Skill level:** Strong technical execution. Weak-to-absent in game design thinking, player psychology, and creative scoping.

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **What they already know** | How to build software. How to scope engineering tasks. That they need some kind of plan before writing code. Basic project management instincts from their day job. |
| **What they are bad at** | Separating "technically cool" from "fun to play." Designing systems from the player's experience backward rather than the code architecture forward. Knowing when a concept is too ambitious for a first game. Evaluating market viability -- they think in features, not in feelings. |
| **What they fear** | Feature-creeping themselves into a graveyard project. Not being taken seriously because they're "just a programmer." Building something technically impressive that nobody enjoys playing. Wasting their limited non-work hours. |
| **Why they buy this product** | It translates their engineering instinct for structure into game design territory. It gives them a design artifact that feels like a spec -- something they already respect as a working format. It closes the gap between "I can build anything" and "I don't know what to build." |
| **What they will NOT pay for** | Anything that talks down to them technically. Anything that feels like a "learn to code games" course (they can already code). Vague creative exercises with no concrete output. Motivational content. |
| **Language that resonates** | "You can build it. But can you design it?" "Turn your game idea into an actual design spec." "Stop architecture-astronauting your first game." "From concept to spec, not concept to tutorial hell." |

### 3. Small Team in Concept Phase (Tertiary / Expansion)

**Who they are.**
2-5 people (friends, game jam teammates, online collaborators) who have decided to make a game together. Often one programmer, one artist, and one "design/everything" person. Early stage -- they have vibes and a pitch doc that's really just a mood board. Age range varies. May or may not have shipped anything together before.

**Skill level:** Collectively moderate-to-strong in execution disciplines. Collectively weak in structured pre-production and alignment.

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **What they already know** | That they work well together (or hope they do). That they need to agree on scope. That someone needs to "own" design. Basic awareness that games fail in pre-production, not production. |
| **What they are bad at** | Getting five people to converge on one vision. Documenting decisions so they stick. Distinguishing between brainstorming and design. Resolving creative disagreements with frameworks instead of arguments. Knowing when concept phase is done and production should start. |
| **What they fear** | The team falling apart over creative disagreements. Three months of "planning" that produces nothing actionable. One person quietly building the wrong thing because the concept was never pinned down. Wasting everyone's limited time. |
| **Why they buy this product** | It's an alignment tool disguised as a design tool. The structured output gives the team something concrete to react to, argue about productively, and ultimately sign off on. It replaces the meeting where everyone talks past each other. At $19-39 it's trivially split across the team. |
| **What they will NOT pay for** | Project management tools (they'll use Trello/Notion/Discord). Team-building exercises. Anything that assumes a solo creator workflow. Anything that doesn't produce a shareable artifact. |
| **Language that resonates** | "Get your whole team on the same page before anyone opens the engine." "One artifact. One vision. No more 'I thought we agreed...'" "Stop brainstorming. Start designing." |

**Why this is tertiary, not primary:** The buyer is usually one person on the team, but the pitch has to justify a group purchase or group adoption. Acquisition is harder -- you need to reach the person who recognizes the alignment problem, which is a narrower funnel than reaching someone who feels their own idea is fuzzy.

### 4. First-Time Creative Lead (Situational)

**Who they are.**
Someone on a small-to-mid indie team who has been promoted or self-appointed into the "creative director" or "lead designer" role for the first time. They were previously a contributor (artist, programmer, writer). Now they need to make and defend design decisions. Age 24-35 typically.

**Skill level:** Strong in their original discipline. Untested in design leadership, stakeholder communication, and concept-to-spec translation.

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **What they already know** | Their craft. What good games feel like. The team's technical constraints. That they're now accountable for the "what" and not just the "how." |
| **What they are bad at** | Structuring their thinking in a way that others can evaluate and build from. Saying "no" with rationale instead of taste. Producing design documentation that isn't just a stream of consciousness. Defending scope decisions to the team. |
| **What they fear** | Being exposed as someone who doesn't know what they're doing. Making a decision that tanks the project. Losing the team's respect. The impostor syndrome is acute and specific. |
| **Why they buy this product** | It's a crutch that looks like a tool. It gives them a process to follow so they don't have to invent one from scratch while already under pressure. The output makes them look organized and thoughtful to their team. |
| **What they will NOT pay for** | Leadership coaching. Anything that explicitly says "for beginners." Anything that doesn't produce output they can show to someone else immediately. |
| **Language that resonates** | "You're leading design now. Here's the playbook." "Produce design artifacts your team can actually build from." "Look like you've done this before." |

**Why this is situational:** The pain is real but the window is narrow. They need this the week they start the role, not before, not six months in. Hard to reach at the right moment with cold acquisition. Better as a secondary conversion path than a primary target.

### 5. Aspiring Game Designer -- Student / Career-Changer (Deprioritized)

**Who they are.**
Game design student, recent grad, or someone in an unrelated career who wants to "break into" game design. Age 18-30. Heavy consumer of GDC talks, game design YouTube, and design theory content. May have completed coursework but has not shipped a real project under real constraints.

**Skill level:** Varies wildly. Some have strong theoretical knowledge with zero practical application. Others are starting from scratch.

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **What they already know** | Game design vocabulary (MDA framework, player typologies, etc.). What a design document is in theory. That the industry is competitive. |
| **What they are bad at** | Applying theory to a specific idea. Making decisions under constraints. Knowing the difference between "interesting design exploration" and "shippable concept." Finishing things. |
| **What they fear** | That they'll never actually make a game. That their ideas aren't good enough. That they lack some secret knowledge that "real" designers have. |
| **Why they might buy** | It feels like a shortcut to legitimacy. "If I fill this out, I'll have a real design document." |
| **Why they're a bad fit** | Their motivation is learning and identity, not output and shipping. They'll buy at $19 but then want a course, community, mentorship, and hand-holding -- none of which this product provides. They generate the most support tickets, the most refund requests ("I didn't understand how to use it"), and the least word-of-mouth to other buyers. The product assumes you have an idea worth sharpening. This audience often doesn't yet. |
| **What they will NOT pay for** | Anything that assumes they already have a clear idea. Anything that doesn't teach them. Anything without video walkthroughs or examples. |
| **Language that resonates (if you were targeting them, which you shouldn't):** | "Learn game design by doing it." "Your first real design document." "From classroom to concept." |

## Public Label Audit

How each label performs when used in copy, ads, community posts, or landing pages.

| Label | Verdict | Reasoning |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| **"idea guy"** | **AVOID -- triggers defensiveness** | In game dev culture, "idea guy" is a pejorative. It means "someone who contributes nothing but thinks their idea is the valuable part." Even people who ARE functionally the idea person on a team will bristle at this label. Using it, even self-deprecatingly, signals that you see your audience as the butt of the joke. Never use this in customer-facing copy. Useful only as internal shorthand. |
| **"solo indie founder"** | **AVOID -- too startup-brained** | "Founder" imports Silicon Valley connotations that most indie game creators actively reject. They don't think of themselves as founders. They're makers, developers, creators. "Founder" implies fundraising, pitch decks, and growth metrics -- the opposite of why most people make indie games. It also sounds pretentious in game dev circles. |
| **"aspiring game designer"** | **AVOID -- signals beginner identity** | "Aspiring" means "not yet." Nobody with a real project in progress calls themselves aspiring. It self-selects for the student/dreamer segment you're deprioritizing. It also frames the audience as lacking rather than building. |
| **"programmer with a game idea"** | **USE -- with care** | This one actually works because it's specific and non-judgmental. Programmers self-identify strongly with "programmer." The framing "with a game idea" is aspirational, not diminishing. It implies capability plus ambition. The risk is that it's too narrow for primary copy -- it excludes artists, writers, and generalists. Use it in targeted ads and specific landing page variants, not in the main headline. |
| **"first-time creative lead"** | **USE -- in specific contexts** | Accurate and non-condescending. "First-time" is honest without being belittling. The problem is reach: most people won't search for or self-identify with this phrase until they're already in the role. Good for blog content, SEO, and targeted outreach. Not a homepage label. |
| **"small team in concept phase"** | **USE -- as a situational qualifier** | Descriptive and neutral. Nobody is offended by this. The limitation is that it describes a situation, not an identity -- people don't wake up thinking "I am a small team in concept phase." Use it to describe who the product is for, not as an identity label in ads. Works well in subheads and feature descriptions. |

### Labels to Use Instead

| Recommended Label | Why It Works |
|---|---|
| **"indie game creator"** | Broad enough to include programmers, artists, designers, and generalists. "Creator" is warmer than "developer" and doesn't gatekeep by technical skill. |
| **"indie dev"** | The actual in-group term. Casual, familiar, zero pretension. Use in community-facing copy. |
| **"solo dev" / "solo creator"** | Specific to the primary ICP. Worn as a badge of honor in the community. |
| **"game dev with an idea they can't stop thinking about"** | Longer but emotionally precise. Works in headlines and ad copy. Describes the feeling, not the job title. |

## Final Recommendation

### Primary ICP: Solo Indie Creator

Build the product, landing page, onboarding, and all primary copy for this person. They are the largest reachable audience, they have the most acute version of the pain, they buy impulsively at this price point, and they generate word-of-mouth in the communities where your other segments also hang out.

**Acquisition channel fit:** Twitter/Bluesky indie dev circles, r/gamedev, itch.io devlogs, indie game Discord servers, game jam communities. These are all places where solo creators already congregate and talk about their projects.

### Secondary ICP: Programmer with a Game Idea

Create a targeted landing page variant or ad set for this segment. The core product doesn't need to change -- the framing does. Emphasize the translation from "I can build anything" to "here's what's worth building." This segment has disposable income (professional developer salary) and is accustomed to buying tools and resources.

**Acquisition channel fit:** Programming subreddits, Hacker News, dev Twitter, gamedev-adjacent coding communities. Some overlap with primary ICP channels but also reaches people who haven't yet entered indie dev spaces.

### Explicit Exclusions

| Exclude | Why |
|---------|-----|
| **Aspiring game designers / students** | Motivation mismatch. They want education, not a tool. They'll drain support resources and leave mediocre reviews because the product "didn't teach them enough." Let them find the product organically later when they have a real project. Do not target them in ads or copy. |
| **AAA / professional studio designers** | They already have internal processes, proprietary templates, and production pipelines. This product is beneath their workflow complexity. Targeting them dilutes positioning. |
| **People without a game idea yet** | The product sharpens an existing idea. It does not generate ideas. If someone arrives without one, the product fails them and they blame the product. The landing page should make this prerequisite explicit: "You have a game idea. This helps you make it real." |

### Language Direction for All Copy

1. **Lead with output, not process.** "Get a pitch-ready design doc" beats "Learn our design methodology."
2. **Use the word "idea" without shame but pair it with action.** "Your game idea, sharpened and stress-tested" -- not "Do you have an idea?" (too basic) or "Validate your concept" (too startup).
3. **Mirror game dev vernacular.** Core loop. Scope. Prototype. Playtest. Vertical slice. Ship. These are the words the audience already uses. Don't invent new terminology.
4. **Never say "aspiring," "wannabe," "beginner," or "learn."** The audience is building something. Talk to them like they're building something.
5. **Acknowledge the real enemy: wasted time.** Not ignorance, not lack of talent -- wasted months on the wrong scope, the wrong mechanic, the wrong assumption. The product saves time, not face.
6. **Tone: competent peer, not guru.** Write like a fellow dev who shipped a few things and figured out a process, not like a design authority dispensing wisdom. The audience trusts practitioners, not educators.
